# Line language

The target looks like someone drew their meal from above with a single ballpoint or fineliner pen on a plain sheet of paper, in one sitting, without erasing.

## Stroke

- One pen, one line color, one stroke weight: about `0.25–0.4%` of the canvas's short side (roughly 3–4 px at 1024 px). Natural pressure may thicken or thin a stroke slightly; never brush-like taper or calligraphic contrast.
- Lines are continuous, unhurried, and gently wobbly. Circles are not compass-perfect: they flatten slightly, bulge, and their start and end often overlap or leave a tiny gap.
- Allow overshoot: a line may run a little past a corner or past where it meets another line.
- Corners are soft and rounded. No ruler-straight lines; long straight edges bow slightly.
- Lines may pass through each other (transparent overlap). A fish laid across a plate shows the plate rim through it; a glass shows its base through its body. Do not erase hidden lines.
- Ink is opaque and even along the stroke. Ballpoint micro-skips are fine; blots, drips, and bleed are not.

## Fill and marks

- Shapes are outlines only. Interiors are paper.
- Each object gets at most one characteristic interior mark family (see `object-vocabulary.md`): a dot ring, a few specks, a spiral, seed dots, a segment star, a stem with a leaf.
- Use at most one or two small **dense hatch accents** in the whole drawing: a tight scribble or parallel hatching filling one small shape (a cheese wedge, a chocolate square, a seaweed strip, a coffee surface). It is the only solid-looking area and should stay under about 1% of the canvas.
- Dots are small, hand-pressed, and slightly irregular in size and spacing. Small ovals ("seeds" or "pebbles") are drawn as tiny open loops.
- No shading, cross-hatched volume, tonal gradients, shadows, highlights, outlines-plus-fill, or color.

## Proportion and viewpoint

- Top-down flat lay. Plates, bowls, and pans are near-circles; boards and trays are soft rectangles; utensils lie flat.
- Naive mixed viewpoint is allowed for glasses and cups: rim loop plus base loop, with the body sides as two gently converging lines.
- Proportions may be slightly off in a charming way (a lemon a bit too big, a fork a bit too long), but each object must stay recognizable from silhouette plus its one mark.
- No vanishing-point perspective, no table edge, no horizon.

## Composition

- Three to nine objects scattered loosely across the sheet, like a flat-lay seen from above.
- Keep generous empty paper: the drawing occupies about 65–80% of the canvas; outer margins are roughly 8–12% of the short side. Nothing is cropped by the edge.
- Most objects do not touch. Allow one or two deliberate overlaps (a utensil resting on a plate, a slice laid across a dish) drawn transparently.
- One hero dish is largest and slightly off-center. Smaller items orbit it with uneven spacing; avoid a rigid grid or perfect symmetry.
- Objects sit at slightly different angles; utensils lie diagonally rather than perfectly vertical.
- Keep the photo's rough arrangement (what is left/right, near/far) but loosen it.

## Paper

- One flat background color across the whole canvas.
- A very faint natural unevenness is welcome: soft, broad tonal drift like a sheet photographed in daylight, and a barely visible paper tooth. Keep it subtle enough that the background still reads as one color.
- No borders, frames, notebook lines, grids, stains, folds, tape, or visible table under the paper. Do not show the paper as an object; the whole canvas is the paper.

## Anti-style

Reject: photo filters, edge detection, traced photo contours, posterization, flat color fills, watercolor washes, marker fills, crayon or pencil texture, charcoal, chalk smudge (except texture-free light-on-dark lines), polished vector line art with perfect circles, uniform machine-perfect stroke, coloring-book bold outlines, comic inking, kawaii faces, botanical or technical illustration detail, 3D, gradients, shadows, and decorative borders.
